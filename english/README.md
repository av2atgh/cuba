# A prospect of Cuba: Past and Future

Three-part book. Parts I and II report the history of Cuba 1959–1990 and
1990–2026 without taking a side — the good and the bad, in the same chapter, at
the same length. Part III is the point of the book: a proposed socio-political
and economic system for Cuba from 2026, built on the country's actual strengths
and answerable to the record the first two parts establish.

Production style follows `~/av2atg/chygraph/book`, which follows
`~/av2atg/LocalNetworkGrowth`: 5×8 in trim, 10pt, grayscale, `fancyhdr`
running heads, one file per chapter, `natbib` author–year citations. The only
preamble additions are two set-off environments built on chygraph's model box —
`ledger` for Parts I and II, `design` (with the `rules`/`\rl` clause list) for
Part III.

## The argument in one paragraph

Cuba has assets that compose: a population that can read and be trained; a
biotechnology and medical establishment with real products and a reputation
still worth money; beaches, reefs and colonial cities that are underexploited;
sunlight falling on a country that burns imported fuel oil; and a diaspora of
about two million. Those assets have been available for twenty years and have
not converted, and the embargo is not a sufficient explanation. What is missing
is the institutional machinery that turns an asset into an investment —
enforceable contracts, a currency that means something, courts a foreigner can
lose in fairly, procurement that publishes what it bought. So the longest
chapters in Part III are not about solar panels; they are about money, property
and corruption.

## The one non-negotiable

Part III retains a **universal welfare state at Western European standard**, and
everything else is arranged around affording it. What changes is the financing,
not the benefit: Cuba's social state was paid for by Soviet terms of trade, then
by Venezuelan oil, then by monetary emission — and an entitlement funded by an
external rent lasts exactly as long as the rent. The proposal is a European
benefit structure on a European tax structure, with the cost of that commitment
— a tax burden well above the Latin American norm — stated rather than hidden.
`welfare.tex` owns the revenue structure; `money.tex` owns stabilisation, and
the two must agree on the tax-to-GDP ratio.

## What Part II establishes

The finding the history is there to produce, and the thing Part III is designed
against: **the binding problem in Cuba is not which policies to adopt but how to
make them irrevocable.** The same sequence runs four times — 1980, 1993–95,
2008–13, 2021. Duress forces a liberalisation; the liberalisation works within
months; the pressure lifts and it is withdrawn (1986, 1996–2004, 2017, 2024–25);
and the withdrawal lands just before it is least affordable. Because everyone
knows the reforms are revocable, nobody invests behind them, which guarantees
they underperform, which supplies the argument for revoking them. Almost every
measure proposed in Part III has already been tried in Cuba. Most worked. All
were reversed. That is why `polity.tex` — credible commitment — comes second in
Part III and not last.

The second finding, on the embargo, which both libraries get wrong by omission:
**the embargo sets the cost of Cuban failure and the Cuban system sets its
probability.** `ledgerch.tex` states both halves at the same length — the
180-day shipping rule, bank de-risking, exclusion from the IFIs and
Helms–Burton's conversion of policy into statute on one side; on the other, that
Cuba may buy from anyone and cannot pay because it has been in default since
1986, and that no foreign statute wrote the wholesale-market ban, *acopio*, or
the licensing of activity by enumerated list.

## What Part III proposes

Twenty-four chapters, 52 numbered design boxes. The positions the book actually
takes, as opposed to the ground it surveys:

- **Rule of law before ballots**, with the electoral calendar entrenched and
  justiciable at the start so that "later" is a date rather than a rationale.
  Association, press and information come first because the institutions need
  them; competitive national elections at year five.
- **Official unilateral dollarisation** as the entry regime — argued not as
  optimal in general but as the only option that needs no reserves, no
  credibility and no discretion, which is exactly Cuba's constraint set. With a
  published exit.
- **Six gigawatts of solar plus eight gigawatt-hours of storage** over a decade,
  with the arithmetic on the page: ~0.08% of the land, $6–8bn, displacing
  ~3 Mt/yr of fuel, five-to-six-year payback. Storage is not optional — the
  Cuban peak is after sunset.
- **Value per visitor as the published tourism metric**, no arrival target, a
  moratorium on state hotel construction, and casas particulares and paladares
  as the default form of the industry rather than its tolerated margin.
- **The right to sell your own work** — any resident may contract with a foreign
  client and be paid into an account they control. This, not visas or bandwidth,
  is the binding constraint on Cuban service exports, and it is a repeal.
- **An independent medicines regulator first**, before partnership or capital,
  because a regulator that is also the producer is why forty years of real
  Cuban science earns almost nothing.
- **Compensation, never restitution in kind** — no occupied dwelling returned —
  with all claims settled inside a single capped debt restructuring.
- **GAESA normalised, not expropriated and not left alone**: civilian ownership,
  published audited accounts, no privileges, divested over a decade, with the
  bargain written down.
- **Remove discretion before selling anything.** Most anti-corruption is
  deregulation: single rates, deemed consent, no negotiated terms. Courts before
  the anti-corruption agency; press before the transparency portal.

The financing conclusion is the one worth arguing with: **the largest available
source of finance is Cuba's own investment budget, spent differently.** Hotel
construction redirected over a decade roughly funds the entire energy programme,
which then saves more per year in foreign exchange than the hotels would have
earned.

`sequence.tex` closes with twelve published indicators — net migration first —
by which the design can be judged to be failing. A proposal that cannot be
falsified is not a proposal.

## Rules the manuscript holds itself to

**Both sides in the same chapter.** Parts I and II never defer the bad news to a
later chapter or the good news to a footnote. Each history chapter closes on a
`ledger` box with *Achieved* and *Cost* set down together.

**Numbers carry their provenance.** See `sources.tex`. Any figure not traced to
a primary source is marked `\unv` in the manuscript and listed in `verify.md`;
the macro prints nothing, so the list is mechanically extractable. Contested
figures appear as ranges with the disagreement named, and where nobody knows,
the text says nobody knows. Peso figures always carry the exchange rate.

**Part III is stated as rules.** A design that cannot be written as a numbered
institutional rule is not a design. Part III's proposals go in `design` boxes
with the `rules` clause list, so each clause can be argued with separately. A
`design` box is always a proposal and never a description of anything that
exists.

**Cross-references by name, not number.** Plan comments refer to other chapters
by file stem (`` `energy' ``), never by number — chapter numbers shift whenever
a chapter is inserted, and one already has.

## Building

```
latexmk -pdf main.tex
```

Current state: **0 errors, 0 undefined references, 0 overfull hboxes, 0 overfull vboxes, 0
BibTeX warnings, 232 pages** — a complete draft: 24 of 24 chapters, 52 numbered
design boxes, 3 figures, 40 citations against 33 references.

**Check vboxes, not just hboxes.** The `design` and `ledger` boxes were
originally chygraph's model box — an `lrbox` in a `minipage`, which cannot break
across a page. Several of Part III's design boxes are longer than a page, so
they ran off the bottom, and an hbox-only check does not see it. They are now
`tcolorbox` with `breakable`, as *Local network growth* does for its calculation
box. The counter is deliberately a plain LaTeX counter stepped *before* the box
opens, rather than tcolorbox's auto counter, so that a `\label` just inside
`\begin{design}` still resolves — which is how every Part III cross-reference is
written.
Re-measure rather than trust these after any edit:

```
grep -c '^!' main.log                       # errors
grep -o 'begin{design}' *.tex | wc -l       # design boxes
grep -o '\\citep' *.tex | wc -l             # citations
grep -c 'Warning' main.blg                  # bibliography warnings
grep -c 'Overfull .hbox' main.log           # lines running into the margin
grep -c 'Overfull .vbox' main.log           # content running off the page
grep -c 'undefined' main.log                # undefined references
grep -n 'unv' *.tex | grep -v '^main.tex'   # unverified figures, cf. verify.md
```

**Trim clearance is not a TeX diagnostic.** A folio sitting too close to the
trim edge is not an overfull box and TeX reports nothing; a print service
rejects it anyway. Measure the PDF instead. On a part or chapter opening — the
`plain` pages, which are the ones that print the folio in the footer:

```
pdftotext -f 13 -l 13 -bbox main.pdf - | python3 -c \
"import re,sys; s=sys.stdin.read(); h=float(re.search(r'height=\"([0-9.]+)\"',s)[1]); \
print(min((h-float(y))/72 for y in re.findall(r'yMax=\"([0-9.]+)\"',s)), 'in from bottom trim')"
```

Should read ≥ 0.5in, the safety area print checks draw. It currently reads
0.506in.
